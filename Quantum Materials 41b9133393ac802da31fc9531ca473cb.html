C. Synthesis-dependent disorder in quantum materials

Especially interesting research gap

This is a direction I think deserves more attention from undergraduate and high-school researchers.

An ideal theoretical crystal is perfectly ordered. Real materials are not.

They contain:

  • Atomic vacancies.
  • Impurities.
  • Strain variations.
  • Lattice defects.
  • Spatially varying chemical composition.

These imperfections can change the properties of quantum materials.

A particularly interesting 2026 perspective in Nature Physics discusses synthesis dependence as a major challenge in quantum materials research. Sample-to-sample variations can produce different observed physical properties. Nature

4. How to incorporate genuine ab initio physics

I want to address this separately because I think it could substantially improve the scientific quality of your project.

You mentioned deriving equations using ab initio elements. That is absolutely possible, although I would avoid attempting a completely new first-principles computational framework.

I would use a three-stage methodology.

Stage 1

Microscopic physical model

Begin with the electronic and ionic Hamiltonian. Establish the approximations and derive the effective interactions you need.

Stage 2

First-principles parameterization

Obtain electronic structures, phonon dispersions, and electron–phonon coupling parameters from published calculations or your own DFT calculations with suitable computational resources.

Stage 3

Independent theoretical investigation

Construct your effective Hamiltonian, derive the relevant approximations, and implement numerical simulations to test your predictions.

Some computational resources worth investigating:

  • Quantum ESPRESSO — DFT and density-functional perturbation theory.
  • Wannier90 — constructing localized Wannier functions and effective electronic models.

I would particularly recommend learning the theoretical foundations of density-functional theory, tight-binding models, and density-functional perturbation theory.

You do not need to run an enormous DFT calculation to make an original theoretical contribution.

1. Quantum acoustics: a potentially excellent research direction

Quantum acoustics studies acoustic waves and mechanical vibrations in the quantum regime.

The central idea is that sound, at sufficiently small energy scales, can be described in terms of quantized vibrational excitations called phonons.

This creates a fascinating connection between:

  • Condensed matter physics.
  • Quantum optics.
  • Nanomechanics.
  • Superconducting circuits.
  • Quantum information.
  • Phononic crystals and metamaterials.

There are several distinct research directions here.

A. Phonon–photon coupling

A phonon is a quantum of mechanical vibration, while a photon is a quantum of electromagnetic radiation.

Potential research question:

How does the spectral structure of a phononic environment influence the coherent transfer of excitations between a mechanical resonator and an electromagnetic cavity?

You could investigate how coupling, detuning, and phonon spectral density influence energy transfer.

An interesting extension would be examining non-Markovian effects, where the environment retains memory of its interactions with the resonator.

That is a much more interesting question than simply simulating energy exchange between two oscillators.

B. Topological phononics

My favorite quantum acoustics direction for you

You are probably familiar with topological insulators in electronic systems.

But topological phenomena can also occur in mechanical and acoustic systems.

A phononic crystal is a periodically structured material that controls how mechanical waves propagate.

By designing the geometry and symmetry of the structure, researchers can produce phononic band structures with topological properties.

This can result in localized edge states that propagate differently from ordinary bulk vibrational modes.

D. Quantum acoustic metamaterials

This is another direction worth investigating.

Metamaterials are engineered structures whose effective properties arise from their geometry and internal structure.

In acoustic metamaterials, you can manipulate the propagation of mechanical waves through the design of the material.

Some research questions:

  • How does quasiperiodicity affect phononic band gaps?
  • Can structural asymmetry produce unusual phonon localization?
  • How do correlated defects affect acoustic transmission?
  • Can mechanical lattice geometry produce unusual vibrational states?

One advantage is that many of these questions can initially be investigated using classical mechanics.

You can then quantize the normal modes and examine their quantum behavior.

That gives you a relatively accessible route into quantum acoustics without having to begin with advanced quantum mechanics.

1. The research directions I would investigate

I want to narrow this to four candidates. These are specific enough to develop into research questions, but broad enough that we can still investigate their originality.

Candidate 1: Phonon-induced fluctuations in topological quantum materials

My first choice to investigate

Proposed research question:

How do spatial and temporal correlations in phonon-induced lattice fluctuations influence the stability of topological electronic phases in two-dimensional materials?

This builds on the earlier idea, but I would make the research question considerably more precise.

Why this is interesting

There are three established bodies of research:

  • Phonons can induce topological phase transitions.
  • Spatially correlated disorder can change topological behavior.
  • Local topological markers can reveal fluctuations near phase transitions.

Importantly, these are already established findings:

  • Saha and Garate (2014), Phonon-induced topological insulation.
  • Antonius and Louie (2016), Temperature-Induced Topological Phase Transitions: Promoted versus Suppressed Nontrivial Topology.
  • Favata, Baù, and Marrazzo (2025), Fluctuations and Correlations of Local Topological Order Parameters in Disordered Two-Dimensional Topological Insulators. APS Journals +2

This means simply studying phonon-induced topological transitions or correlated disorder would not be original.

Where I would look for a contribution

Investigate the combined effect of:

  • The spatial correlation length of lattice fluctuations.
  • The temporal correlation time of those fluctuations.
  • The electronic system’s proximity to a topological phase transition.

One possible hypothesis is that the temporal and spatial characteristics of lattice fluctuations affect local topological stability differently, even when their average perturbation strength is identical.

A possible computational framework:

You could calculate:

  • Local Chern markers.
  • Electronic localization.
  • Bulk band-gap evolution.
  • Time-dependent electronic response.
  • Correlation functions of local topological quantities.

My assessment: This has substantial theoretical potential. However, it is not yet a verified research gap. The 2025 paper makes a generic disorder-correlation project particularly difficult to defend, so the dynamic phonon component would need to provide a demonstrably new contribution.

Candidate 2: Quantum geometry and electron–phonon interactions

Potentially the strongest mathematical project

Proposed research question:

How does the quantum geometry of electronic bands influence phonon-mediated electronic transport in two-dimensional quantum materials?

This is an area I particularly want you to investigate.

Quantum geometry describes geometric properties of quantum states as they vary across momentum space.

Two important quantities are:

  • Berry curvature.
  • Quantum metric.

Together, these are related to the quantum geometric tensor.

Here, gij​ is the quantum metric and Ωij​ is the Berry curvature, using one common convention.

These quantities have implications for superconductivity, optical responses, transport, and other material properties.

The potential research gap

A question worth investigating is whether quantum geometry introduces additional structure into electron–phonon scattering and transport that is not captured by simpler band-energy-based approximations.

For example, compare two electronic models with similar energy dispersions but different quantum geometries.

Would their phonon-mediated transport properties differ?

You could investigate this through:

  • Constructing two-band Hamiltonians.
  • Deriving their eigenstates.
  • Calculating Berry curvature and quantum metric.
  • Introducing electron–phonon coupling.
  • Computing scattering rates or transport coefficients.
  • Identifying relationships between geometric quantities and transport.

A 2025 review by Yu and colleagues, Quantum Geometry in Quantum Materials, is an excellent starting point. DOI

Another useful paper is:

Non-trivial quantum geometry and the strength of electron–phonon coupling (2024).

It provides a concrete example of why the geometry of electronic states matters when considering electron–phonon interactions.

My assessment: This might be your best option if you want to derive equations and develop an original mathematical model. It is also a good way to move beyond simply running simulations.

The challenge is that quantum geometry and electron–phonon interactions are both active research areas. You would need to identify a very specific new prediction.

Candidate 3: Non-equilibrium phonons and electronic transport

Proposed research question:

How does a non-equilibrium phonon distribution modify electronic transport in low-dimensional materials compared with equilibrium phonon assumptions?

This is an interesting computational physics problem.

Many conventional transport calculations begin with equilibrium phonon distributions.

But under certain conditions, phonons may not remain in equilibrium.

For example:

  • Strong external excitation.
  • Temperature gradients.
  • Ultrafast optical excitation.
  • Spatially nonuniform heating.

You could investigate the consequences of relaxing the equilibrium assumption.

You would investigate how different phonon distributions affect the collision term and the resulting transport.

Possible contribution

Develop a reduced theoretical model that predicts the conditions under which equilibrium phonon approximations become inaccurate.

You could compare your model against established transport calculations.

The 2025 review by Claes and colleagues, Phonon-limited electronic transport through first principles, is especially useful here. It discusses Boltzmann, Kubo, and Landauer–Büttiker approaches. Nature

Candidate 4: Exciton–phonon coupling in two-dimensional quantum materials

Proposed research question:

How do the symmetry and dispersion of phonon modes influence exciton coherence and relaxation in two-dimensional semiconductors?

An exciton is a bound electron–hole pair.

The interesting part is that excitons interact with lattice vibrations, which can influence their energy, lifetime, and coherence.

You could investigate:
